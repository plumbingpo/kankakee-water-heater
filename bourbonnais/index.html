How Bourbonnais Homeowners Can Extend Water Heater Life

The best way to avoid a surprise leak is routine maintenance. We recommend that homeowners flush the tank once a year (more often with hard water), have the anode rod checked every three to five years, test the relief valve once a year to make sure it isn't stuck, and keep the thermostat around 120 degrees, which limits scalding risk and slows mineral buildup. Tankless owners should plan on a descaling flush every year if their water is hard.

How old is your water heater? The serial number on the label usually includes the month and year it was built, and that date says a lot about what comes next. Most tank heaters make it to 8 to 12 years, heat pump units 10 to 15, and regularly descaled tankless units around 20. If your heater is beyond that point and also shows rusty water, rumbling, or dampness under the tank, it's usually due for a planned replacement, which is almost always cheaper and less stressful than a rushed emergency install after a leak.

If warning signs do show up, acting quickly keeps them small. A dripping relief valve might point to excess pressure or thermal expansion with nowhere to go, both inexpensive fixes. The same drip left alone for a season can keep stressing the tank until a seam gives way, and a small repair becomes a full replacement plus water damage. It's smart to keep our number saved on your phone so you can reach us at the first sign of trouble. Early intervention saves time, money, and ruined floors.

What Goes Wrong With Local Water Heaters

Water heaters in and around Bourbonnais deal with a predictable set of stresses. Depending on whether a home is on municipal water or a private well, hard water may be a real factor: calcium and magnesium settle to the bottom of a tank as sediment, forcing the burner or lower element to work harder, and that's what causes the rumbling and popping many homeowners hear when the heater fires. Tankless units aren't immune either: scale builds inside the heat exchanger, which is why annual descaling matters so much in hard-water areas.

Aging equipment is the other half of the story. A tank-style water heater usually runs 8 to 12 years, and by then the anode rod is often used up, leaving the steel exposed to corrosion. The area has a mix of fuel types, natural gas and propane units in some homes and electric in others, so our technicians handle everything from worn thermocouples and sooty burners to tripped high-limit switches and dead elements.

Homes on private wells can bring iron, sulfur, and grit into the tank, and a rotten-egg odor in the hot water usually points to a reaction with the anode rod that a swap to a different anode type can solve. Older installations often need code updates when a heater is replaced: an expansion tank on closed systems, a correctly piped T&P discharge line, a drain pan where a leak would hit finished floors, and earthquake straps where local code requires them. All of these is fixable, and most are much cheaper when caught early.
